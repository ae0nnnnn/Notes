# Measures of Financial Performance

## Balance Sheets

A Balance Sheet is a snapshot of the financial position of a company at particular moment in time.

The balance sheet provides a measure of a business' assets (what it owns) and a business's liabilities (what they owe).

Since 2009, the term balance sheet was replaced with the term **Statement of Financial Position** but for the exam Balance sheet would be used 

Balance sheets help **stakeholders** to understand how a business has raised capital and uses to which this capital has been put.

 The People Interested with this 
- Investors - Concerned about risk and return in relation to their investments. They require information to decide whether they should  continue to invest in a business and check if the business will still be able to pay dividends
  
- Lenders - Will loans be paid when due?
  
- Creditors - What is the short-term liquidity of the business? Is the business able to pay short-term debt when it falls due?
  
- Customers - Will the business continue? They may have a long-term interest in the company's range of products and services
  
- Employees - Employees require information about the stability and continuing profitability of the business. They are interested in employment prospects, maintenance of pension funding and retirement benefits. They are also likely to be interested in the pay and benefits obtained by senior management.
  
- Government - HMRC needs information on business profitability in order to level and collect Corporation Tax. Various regulatory agencies (e.g. the Competition Commission and the Environment Agency) need information to support decisions about taker overs and grants.
  
- Analysts - Investment analysts are an important user group - Specifically for companies quoted on a stock exchange. They require very detailed financial and other information in order to analyse the competitive performance of a business and its sector.
  
- Media




### Why do Balance Sheets Balance?
The Value of assets held by a business must always be match by a combination of their liabilities and equity.

A standard company balance sheet has three parts: assets, liabilities, and owner's equity or shareholders' capital.

The Balance sheet has two sides that must be equal or balance each other out. The logic behind it is that a company must pay for its assets by borrowing money from lenders or though investors → This is the concept of double-entry

### How to Calc things on the Balance Sheet

#### Fixed Assets 
All you need to do is to add up the value of each of the fixed asset to get **Total Fixed Assets**.

#### Current Assets 
Same thing as above add up all the values of each current assets to get **Total Current Assets**.
#### Current Liabilities
Same thing the past two all you need to do is to add up the value of each current liability to get **Total Current Liabilities**

#### Working Capital 
Current Assets - Current Liabilities = **Working Capital**

#### Net Assets 
Working Capital + Fixed Assets = **Net Assets**
Or 
(Current Assets + Non-Current (Fixed) Assets) – (Current Liabilities + Non-Current (Long-Term) Liabilities) = **Net Assets**


#### Capital Employed 

You would need to add up the financed by column to get **Capital Employed** the idea is that it should Balance with **Net Assets**

### Term Definitions

#### Assets 

These are what the business **owns**.

There are two main types of asset held by a business:
- Fixed Assets (or non-current)
- Current Assets
  
The length of **time** that an asset proves useful to a business determines whether it's a classified as fixed or current.

##### Fixed Assets 
- These have a **useful life of 12 months or more**.
- These Assets are needed to help the business function effectively in the long-term and are **not easy to turn into cash quickly**.
- They are not purchased with the intent of immediate resale but rather for productive use within the business.
- Fixed assets are also commonly known in accounting term as **PP&E** or property, plant, and equipment.


These Include: 

- Buildings 
- Land 
- Machinery 
- Vehicles 
- Brands
- Furniture and fittings 


Valuing Fixed Assets
- The value of fixed assets changes regularly and is influenced by their usage (tangible assets) and brand value (intangible assets).
- When tangible assets are reduced in value this is known as depreciation.
- The value of a fixed asset as shown on the balance sheet is known as it **net book value**. The accurate reflection of an assets value is important so that investors are not mislead and the company is overvalued.
##### Current Assets 
These are assets a business owns which are either cash, the equine of cash or are expected to the turned into cash during the next twelve months.

These have a useful life of less than 12 months.

These assets are needed to help a business to function effectively in the short term and are easily turned into cash. 

The easier that asset can be converted into cash the more liquid it is. 

###### Inventories (Stock)

Inventory is classified into 3 distinct categories 
- Raw materials 
- Work in Progress 
- Finished Goods


Finished goods are often the easiest to turn into cash.

Some types of finished goods are perishable and are far less liquid than goods with a longer shelf life e.g. food.

The least liquid form of inventory is work-in-progress as these are unfinished goods and of little value until completed.

##### Debtors(Receivables)
This is money that is owed to a business, and it excepted to be repaid (received) within one year.

Most business is B2B, and they would offer credit to customers.

Business have to accept that in using this method of finance, that there is a risk of the debt not been repaid.

When the debt us not repaid it is then classified as a **bad debt**.

Firms should make allowances for bad debts in order to arrive at more accurate value for their  current assets and in particular their debtors 

#### Liabilities 

Liabilities are basically anything that a business **OWES**. It is a debt.

There are three main categories of liability held by a business:

- Current Liabilities 
- Non-Current Liabilities
- Total Equity (Shareholder's Funds)

Liabilities help show the sources of capital that a business have raised in order to purchase their assets.

##### Current Liabilities 

Current Liabilities are debts that are due for repayment by a business with 12 months 
  
As with all liabilities, these are debts that might be owed to a bank, private investor or supplier.

These are :

- Short Term Loans - Repayment of debt and interest charges
- Accounts Payable (Trade Credit) - Paying for goods brought on credit 
- Over Drafts
##### Long Term Liabilities 

Long Term (non- current) liabilities are debts that are due for repayment by a business after at least one year.

Investors are interested in a company's non-current liabilities because they want to see if it doesn't have too much debt relative to its cash flwo .


These Include:
- Bank Loans
- Commercial Mortgages 
- Long Term Lease Agreements 
- Debentures


#### Net Assets

The Value of the business at a given point in time is best represented by the Net Assets  figure.

This is calculated by subbing total Liabilities away from total assets. This figure must balance with the total equity value.

#### Working Capital

Working Capital is the money required for the day-to-day operations of the business.

This is the formula → [Working Capital (Formula)](<#Working Capital>)

Working Capital is a good measure of a firm's short term financial health, providing an indication of whether a business holds enough current assets to over their current liabilities.

If a firm holds more current liabilities than current assets then they might struggle to repay debts and could eventually face closure.


Investors can use working capital calcs to assess the operation efficiency of a business for example , are they holding too much cash and therefore not using it efficiently by missing out on investing the money in additional assets.

##### Factors affecting working capital.

1. Needs to hold inventories - Some business needs to hold substantial inventories to meet customer needs e.g - retailers and distributors 
   
2. Production lead time - A product that is made and sold within a short time (e.g. fresh food) requires much less inventory than one where the production process takes a long time (e.g. production of mature cheese)
   
3. Lean Production - Business that successfully implement lean production techniques find that they need to hold significantly less inventory.
   
4. Customer Credit period - In some industries its is expected that a long credit period can be taken before trade debtors need to settle their invoices - which will mean higher working capital would be required.
   
5. Effectiveness of Credit Control - A poorly managed credit control department will allow customers to take too much credit and take too long to settle their bills - which will mean higher trader debtors and higher working capital.
   
6. Supplier credit period - The longer the credit offered by suppliers the better cash flow and working capital.


##### Working Capital Problems.
- Overtrading (funding expansion or growth with working capital)
- Buying things that are not needed.
- Stockpiling 
- Allowing too much credit to suppliers.
- Failure to control debtors.
- Over Borrowing 
- Unpredictable expenditure 
- Unexpected demand changes.
- Seasonal Factors
- Poor financial management.


##### How can WC problems be resolved?

- Lower Stock
- Tight credit control 
- Delay paying suppliers
- Discounts to customers for early payment 
- A good relationship with the bank
- Sale and Leaseback

It is assumed that most fixed (non-current) assets will decline in value over time as they are used (**wear and tear**), or become outdated (**obsolete**), or are no longer in demand.

Depreciation involves recognizing that fixed assets are not worth the same value thought their useful life
